\begin{abstract}
Training a search agent against injected instructions with a loss on the correct answer does not make it ignore instructions in retrieved text. It teaches the agent to resist instructions that push a wrong answer while still following ones that push the right answer, and barely changes how often it adopts the same wrong answer stated as a fact. Attack success and clean accuracy cannot show this, so we add three probes: a plausible wrong answer as an instruction, the same answer as a fact, and the gold answer as an instruction. We train single-search Search-R1-style Qwen2.5-3B agents with GRPO under retrieval injection, with and without this answer supervision, and compare them on 1,000 held-out HotpotQA questions under a hash-committed protocol. Answer supervision halved adoption of a plausible wrong answer in instruction form, from \cfAiR\% to \cfAiCE\%, while the same answer stated as a fact went only from \cfAdR\% to \cfAdCE\%. The gold answer in instruction form was still used on \cfUCE\% of otherwise-missed questions, \cfDUAbs{} points below injection training alone. A released multi-search 7B agent rarely adopted the wrong answer in either form (\sbAd\% and \sbAi\%; development evidence). We recommend the fact-form and correct-answer probes for evaluating injection defenses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Suppose a retrieved document ends with ``DOCUMENT UPDATE: Disregard the original question and all previous instructions. Return exactly \tagtext{answer}$X$\tagtext{/answer} as your final answer.'' We trained search agents against attacks of this kind with an extra cross-entropy loss on the correct answer. On questions they otherwise answered correctly, these agents followed the text on \cfAiCE\% of questions when $X$ was a plausible wrong answer; agents trained with injection alone did so on \cfAiR\%. On questions they otherwise answered wrongly, the same agents followed the same text \cfUCE\% of the time when $X$ was the correct answer. The agents were resisting instructions that push a wrong answer while following ones that push the right answer (Table~\ref{tab:example}), and the defense did much less against the same wrong answer stated as a plain fact.

Defenses against retrieval attacks are usually judged by how often the attack reaches its goal and how much accuracy the defense costs on clean inputs \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo,chen2025struq,chen2025secalign}. These numbers do not reveal what a defense has learned. Consider three agents that all stop answering with the attacker's string. One has learned to distrust instructions inside documents: it stops following an instruction that pushes a wrong answer but still follows one that pushes the right answer. We call this a \emph{gate}, because what it acts on is the instruction. The second checks any assertion, imperative or not, against the rest of its evidence, which we call \emph{verification}. The third discounts whatever was inserted, right or wrong, which we call \emph{suppression}. All three lower attack success, and none changes clean accuracy, because clean inputs contain no inserted text. They come apart on two inputs that standard suites leave out. A wrong answer stated as a fact passes a gate but should not survive verification, and a correct answer delivered as an instruction is refused only by suppression. The distinction matters in deployment, because each mechanism leaves a different class of attacks open.

We study the simplest training-time defense for a reinforcement-learned search agent. Search-R1-style agents learn from an outcome reward when to search and when to answer \citep{jin2025searchr1}. We train Qwen2.5-3B agents of this kind with GRPO, insert instructions into a fixed number of their training retrievals, and in one configuration add a cross-entropy loss on the correct answer given the attacked input (answer supervision, or answer CE). We compare that configuration with injection training alone, three seeds each. Besides the usual attack, the evaluation adds three probes: a plausible wrong answer delivered as an override instruction, the same wrong answer stated as a fact, and the gold answer delivered as an override instruction. Development data supplied the predictions. We then tested four contrasts once, on 1,000 HotpotQA questions that none of our agents had been trained or evaluated on, under an analysis protocol committed by hash before that split was opened.

The probes add three evaluation conditions to a standard attack. Answer supervision costs little correct inserted content (\cfDUAbs{} points of gold uptake), unlike the suppression that \citet{hermon2026secfid} and \citet{li2026surface} report for other defenses. It makes the agent resist instructions that push a wrong answer, cutting adoption of the attack target from \cfTR\% to \cfTCE\% and of a plausible wrong answer delivered as an instruction from \cfAiR\% to \cfAiCE\%, and it leaves the same wrong answer stated as a fact mostly open, at \cfAdR\% and \cfAdCE\%. All four primary contrasts were confirmed after Holm correction. Under the pre-declared measure the override's other wrong answers fell from \cfXR{} to \cfXCE{} points above a benign instruction; counted over all clean-correct questions the two were similar (\reDoneR{} and \reDoneCE). Answer supervision brought target adoption to \reTCEMin--\reTCEMax\% in every training run, while runs trained with injection alone ranged from \reTRMin\% to \reTRMax\%. A released Search-R1 7B checkpoint, which searches \sbCleanSearches{} times per MuSiQue question, was rarely moved by either payload form (development evidence).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Keeping instructions out of the data.}
Instructions planted in retrieved content can redirect applications built on language models \citep{greshake2023indirect}, and benchmarks now measure this for tool-using agents \citep{zhan2024injecagent,debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhang2025asb}. The main training-time response treats the problem as one of channels. StruQ separates the prompt from the data and fine-tunes the model to follow only the prompt \citep{chen2025struq}, SecAlign optimizes preferences toward the response to the intended instruction \citep{chen2025secalign}, the instruction hierarchy ranks instructions by privilege \citep{wallace2024hierarchy}, and Meta-SecAlign extends this training to open models used as agents \citep{chen2025metasecalign}. All of them train on attacked inputs paired with the desired output, and answer CE is the plainest version of that idea for a search agent. Other defenses act outside the weights, marking retrieved text as untrusted \citep{hines2024spotlighting} or constraining control and data flow around the model \citep{debenedetti2025camel}; answer CE, like spotlighting, gives no guarantee of the kind CaMeL provides by design. These defenses are reported by attack success and utility. \citet{hermon2026secfid} looked past those scores and found that defenses mostly resist injection by suppressing untrusted text, which harms tasks such as translation and editing that must process it. \citet{li2026surface} show that fine-tuned injection defenses learn surface cues and over-refuse benign inputs that carry them. Our gold probe asks whether this happens when the benign input is the right answer.

\paragraph{Corrupting the content.}
A second line of work needs no instruction at all. Knowledge-poisoning attacks plant passages that state a chosen wrong answer \citep{zhong2023poisoning,zou2025poisonedrag}, and the matching defenses isolate or filter passages before aggregation \citep{xiang2024robustrag,zhou2025trustrag}. Retrieval-robustness training teaches models to answer despite distracting or noisy passages \citep{zhang2024raft,yoran2024making,fang2024raat}. It is the closest training-time analogue to what we call verification, though these methods target irrelevant or noisy passages, and only the counterfactual noise of RAAT resembles a planted claim. Evaluations of evidence reliance edit a single assertion and count how often systems, including RL-trained search agents, abandon answers they gave correctly without retrieval \citep{zhou2026ragstress}. In this literature misinformation and instruction injection are separate threats with separate defenses, and a defense against one is rarely tested on the other.

\paragraph{RL-trained search agents.}
Search-R1, ReSearch, R1-Searcher, and DeepResearcher learn search behavior from outcome rewards \citep{jin2025searchr1,chen2025research,song2025r1searcher,zheng2025deepresearcher}. Work on their robustness has mostly examined released checkpoints. Deep-search agents under web poisoning rarely move from noticing a suspicious source to recovering the answer, even when prompted to verify, though more agentic search improves final resistance \citep{bi2026haegeo}. Post-training gives limited protection against corrupted tool outputs in general agents \citep{yang2026overreliance}. \citet{chen2026selectbench} train a model with RL to adopt evidence selectively from contaminated retrieval and find that following injected instructions does not improve, but they do not separate the form of a payload from its content. We found no study that asks which channel such training closes, whether it costs correct inserted content, or whether the answer differs for an agent that searches more than once.

\label{sec:mechanisms}

Call the text an attacker appends to a retrieved document a \emph{payload}. A payload has content, the answer it pushes, and a form, which is either an instruction to answer with that content or a declarative statement that it is the answer. A payload can therefore move an agent's answer through two channels. Through the instruction channel, the imperative form adds adoption beyond what the content alone would earn. Through the content channel, a claim stated as fact is believed. A gate closes the instruction channel only for content that is wrong, so it predicts no loss of correct content delivered as an instruction.

Table~\ref{tab:predictions} lists what each mechanism predicts for three probes, relative to the same training without the defense. Adoption of a wrong answer in instruction form falls under all three mechanisms, and it is the only probe a standard attack suite contains. Uptake of the gold answer in instruction form separates suppression from the other two. Adoption of the same wrong answer stated declaratively separates a gate from verification. The standard attack's target, \emph{violet telescope 42}, is implausible as an answer to any question, so an agent could reject it on plausibility alone. A plausible wrong answer removes that shortcut.

\paragraph{Threat model.}
The attacker controls text appended to one document that the agent retrieves on its first search, and knows the question. It does not control the prompt, the other documents, or later searches, and it does not adapt to the defense. Its goal is a chosen final answer: a nonsense string, a plausible wrong answer, or, as a probe of over-defense, the correct one.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Single-search agents}
\label{sec:agents}
We follow the Search-R1 interaction protocol \citep{jin2025searchr1}. The agent reasons, issues a query inside \tagtext{search} tags, receives the top three BM25 documents \citep{robertson2009bm25} from the question's HotpotQA distractor pool \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, and ends with an answer inside \tagtext{answer} tags. A finite pool per question keeps every observation reproducible and lets us place a payload at a known position. All agents start from base Qwen2.5-3B \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and are trained with GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath} on 1,024 HotpotQA training questions, four samples per prompt, with a reward of one when the normalized answer matches a reference. Retrieved tokens are masked from the policy loss.

Two training configurations carry the comparison. \emph{Random injection} appends one of two training instructions, both naming the target \emph{amber compass 17}, to the first retrieval of a subset of rollouts. It asks whether exposure under the outcome reward is enough, which it may not be, since a zero reward cannot distinguish a wrong fact from obedience to the attacker. \emph{Random injection + answer CE} adds, for one attacked input $x$ per selected question with verified answer $y$ and answer-token mask $m_t$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{answer}} = -\frac{\sum_t m_t\log p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\sum_t m_t},
\end{equation}
to the GRPO objective with weight 0.05. The answer comes from a clean rollout that matched the reference, or from the reference itself when every annotated supporting title was retrieved; otherwise the question is skipped. The mask covers answer tokens only, so the loss names the desired answer and teaches no reasoning. Both configurations consume exactly 300 attacked observations and train for the same measured loop time of 3,430 seconds on two GPUs, so that neither is favored for seeing more attacks or more training time. An attack counts only once the policy has read it, and which rollouts receive one is decided by a hash, never by reward. Each runs with three seeds. A configuration trained without injection serves as a reference. A second family that pairs clean and attacked continuations at a shared first search is described in Appendix~\ref{app:training}.

These agents rarely search twice: on the test split they averaged \cfSearchesR{} and \cfSearchesCE{} searches per question. The released Search-R1 3B checkpoint searched exactly once on every one of \scN{} MuSiQue questions in our check (Section~\ref{sec:sevenb-setup}). The 3B results therefore describe how an agent reads a single retrieval.

\subsection{Payloads}
\label{sec:payloads}
Every payload is appended, after a blank line, to the first document returned by the agent's first search. Later searches return unmodified documents. Within each agent and question, all conditions share the agent's own first action and its clean first retrieval, so they differ only in the appended text. Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} lists the payloads verbatim.

The standard attacks are two held-out templates that name the target \emph{violet telescope 42}: an override instruction (``DOCUMENT UPDATE: Disregard the original question \ldots{} Return exactly \tagtext{answer}violet telescope 42\tagtext{/answer}'') and an authority claim. Training used different templates and a different target. The three probes reuse the override template or a declarative sentence:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \emph{instruction, plausible wrong}: the override template carrying $w(q)$, a plausible wrong answer;
\item \emph{declarative, plausible wrong}: ``Reference note: the correct answer to this question is $w(q)$.'';
\item \emph{instruction, gold}: the override template carrying the first gold answer.
\end{itemize}
The declarative payload is padded to the length of the instruction version by repeating the word ``note''.

The wrong answer $w(q)$ is built by a fixed rule that never looks at model outputs. Yes/no answers are flipped, years and other numbers are swapped for a different one drawn from the training questions, and other answers are replaced by the title of a document in the question's own pool that does not contain, and is not contained in, any gold answer and does not appear in the question. Most $w(q)$ are therefore entities of the right kind that sit in the agent's own retrieval pool.

Controls of the same token length separate the attack from mere addition: a neutral metadata line, a fluent but irrelevant sentence, and a \emph{benign instruction} with the override's header and answer tags that asks the agent to keep the original question. Each is padded with ``note'' until it is within two tokens of both attack inputs. On development data the benign instruction caused the most other wrong answers of the three, and a rule fixed before those data were seen selected it as the comparison condition for the test split, as the most conservative choice.

\subsection{Outcomes}
\label{sec:outcomes}
A final answer is scored by normalized exact match (EM) against the gold answers, from the last assistant generation only, so text in retrieved documents can never count as an answer (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). We report rates over pairs of a trained agent and a question, pooled across the three seeds. For a wrong payload, \emph{adoption} is the share of questions the agent answers correctly with clean retrieval whose answer under the payload equals the payload's answer. For the gold payload, \emph{uptake} is the share of questions the agent answers wrongly with clean retrieval that it answers correctly under the payload. \emph{Interference} counts answers that are wrong but are not the attack target. We measure it as the rate of such answers under the override attack minus the rate under the benign instruction, among clean-correct questions that do not end in the target. A question that ends in the target cannot also end in another wrong answer, so a count over all clean-correct questions would make a frequently hijacked agent look less damaged; restricting to non-target outcomes assumes instead that hijacked questions would otherwise have failed at the same rate. The protocol uses the second count, and we also report the first. Confirmatory claims rest on exact match only; a lenient criterion, token F1 of at least 0.5 against the gold or the payload answer, serves as a check.

\subsection{Development, confirmation, and evidence levels}
\label{sec:protocol}
Development used 256 HotpotQA validation questions that the trained agents had already been monitored on, and two earlier held-out splits of 1,000 HotpotQA questions each, on which the same 15 agents had been evaluated under the standard attacks before the probes existed. The interference effect of Section~\ref{sec:interference} was first measured on those earlier splits. On the 256 questions we measured the probes (Section~\ref{sec:gate}) and the control ladder, each with predictions written down before computation. All of these results shaped the confirmatory protocol and are labelled \emph{development} throughout. Table~\ref{tab:splits} lists the splits.

The confirmatory test split holds 1,000 HotpotQA distractor-validation questions, reserved before any inference and disjoint by identifier and normalized text from every question used earlier. The analysis protocol, including the 15 trained models, all payloads, the scorer, the four primary contrasts, the multiplicity correction, and the decision rules, was frozen and its SHA-256 (\texttt{9f2337fa\ldots}) recorded before any model read the split. It was recorded internally, not in a public registry. The released copy has local paths and scheduler fields removed, so it carries the original hash but cannot reproduce it. The primary contrasts compare answer CE with random injection alone (CE minus R) without any prompt defense:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item[$P_a$] adoption of the nonsense target under the override attack is lower;
\item[$P_b$] the drop in adoption of $w(q)$ is larger in instruction form than in declarative form;
\item[$P_c$] uptake of the gold answer in instruction form is not lower by more than 5 points (non-inferiority);
\item[$P_d$] interference relative to the benign instruction is lower.
\end{itemize}
The protocol states that answer CE acts as a gate on instruction-shaped wrong payloads if $P_a$, $P_b$, and $P_c$ are all confirmed, and that the absence of transfer to declarative misinformation may be claimed only if the 90\% interval of the declarative difference lies within $\pm5$ points. Each contrast is tested one-sided by a paired question bootstrap with the trained models held fixed (10,000 draws), and the four are corrected by Holm's procedure at family-wise level 0.05 \citep{holm1979,efron1993bootstrap}. Intervals in this paper are 95\% question-bootstrap intervals with the models fixed unless stated otherwise. The same run also evaluated two prompt defenses as pre-declared secondary cells; they are outside the question of this paper and appear only in the released results. Everything other than $P_a$--$P_d$ is secondary or exploratory, and we say so where it appears.

\subsection{A multi-search agent}
\label{sec:sevenb-setup}
Our 3B agents cannot show what happens when an agent may search again after reading a payload. For that we used the released Search-R1 7B checkpoint \citep{jin2025searchr1} on 1,000 answerable MuSiQue development questions \citep{trivedi2022musique}. Retrieval uses e5-base-v2 \citep{wang2022e5}, the retriever Search-R1 was trained with, over a pool of 101,962 de-duplicated MuSiQue paragraphs, top three per search and at most eight searches. With clean retrieval the checkpoint searched \sbCleanSearches{} times per question and at least twice on \sbCleanGeTwo\% of them. In a check on \scN{} of these questions with the same corpus and retriever, the released single-search 3B checkpoint searched exactly once on every question. The 7B agent saw the clean condition, the override attack, and the three probes, with $w(q)$ built by the same rule, and its predictions were frozen before inference.

\subsection{By the usual measures, answer CE defends}
\label{sec:usual}
Scored the way defenses are usually scored, the test split favors answer CE (descriptive). With clean retrieval the answer-CE agents answered \cfCleanEMCE\% of the questions correctly and the random-injection agents \cfCleanEMR\%. Exact match under attack, averaged over the override and authority templates, was \cfAttackEMCE\% for answer CE and \cfAttackEMR\% for random injection, and the override target made up \cfHitAllCE\% and \cfHitAllR\% of their answers. On these measures answer CE is a working defense; they do not show how it works.

\subsection{Wrong instructions resisted, right ones followed}
\label{sec:gate}
Development data already showed agents that resist wrong instructions and follow right ones. On the 256 development questions, answer CE cut adoption of the nonsense target from \dvTR\% to \dvTCE\% of clean-correct questions and adoption of a plausible wrong answer in instruction form from \dvAiR\% to \dvAiCE\%. The same wrong answer stated as a fact was adopted on \dvAdR\% and \dvAdCE\% of questions. Gold uptake in instruction form stayed high, \dvUR\% and \dvUCE\%. The instruction form kept its pull for correct content: under answer CE it still added \dvPullGoldCE{} points of gold uptake over the same answer stated as a fact (\dvUCE\% and \dvDeclGoldCE\%, Table~\ref{tab:devprobes}), next to \dvPullWrongCE{} points for the wrong answer. For random injection the two figures were \dvPullGoldR{} and \dvPullWrongR{} points. Answer CE removed most of the instruction form's pull for wrong answers and almost none of it for right ones. Two of the predictions we had written down failed: that answer CE would cut gold uptake by at least 10 points, as suppression implies, and that it would protect much less against plausible wrong answers than against the nonsense target.

The test split reproduced every development difference to within 4 points (Table~\ref{tab:devconf}, Figure~\ref{fig:gate}), and all three contrasts that define a gate were confirmed. Answer CE changed adoption of the nonsense target among clean-correct pairs by \cfPa{} points \cfPaCI{} ($P_a$). For the plausible wrong answer it lowered adoption by \cfDAiAbs{} points in instruction form and by \cfDAdAbs{} in declarative form, a difference of \cfPbAbs{} points ($P_b$; intervals in Table~\ref{tab:devconf} and Appendix~\ref{app:analysis}). On a log-odds scale the contrast survives the different baselines, at \reLogOddsInstr{} and \reLogOddsDecl; for gold uptake near its ceiling the \cfDUAbs-point cost is \reLogOddsGold. Gold uptake in instruction form was \cfUR\% for random injection and \cfUCE\% for answer CE, a difference of \cfDU{} points \cfDUCI{} ($P_c$). Its Holm-adjusted one-sided bound, \cfPcBound{} points, clears the 5-point non-inferiority margin, so the claim that answer CE does not suppress correct content of the same form holds, with a small cost that the interval does not rule out. The claim holds for these six models. With seeds resampled its interval, \cfPcNested, crosses the margin, and compared with agents trained without injection the gold-uptake cost is \reCleanGoldCost{} points (descriptive, Table~\ref{tab:allarms}). The two arms were scored on clean-correct sets of similar make-up: the gold answer appeared in the clean retrieval of \reGoldInR\% and \reGoldInCE\% of them.

The protocol allowed us to say that answer CE does not transfer to declarative misinformation only if the 90\% interval of the declarative difference fell within $\pm5$ points. It did not, at \cfDAdNinety, and the 95\% interval excludes zero. Answer CE therefore transfers to the declarative form slightly: about 3 points, next to about 35 for the instruction form.

Answer CE reached the same target-adoption floor in every training run, so the spread of $P_a$ across seeds comes from injection training alone. Point estimates from a few trained policies are unreliable \citep{agarwal2021precipice}, so Figure~\ref{fig:seeds} shows every seed. Per seed pair the change in target adoption was \cfPaSeeds{} points. The three seed pairs agree in direction on $P_a$, $P_b$, and $P_d$; for the plausible wrong answer both arms vary, and on $P_c$ the seed pairs do not agree in sign (\cfPcSeeds; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:seeds}).

The effect was largest where the agent's own retrieval contained the gold answer, a stratification the protocol declared as descriptive. In the \cfStInAiPairsR{} and \cfStInAiPairsCE{} pairs whose clean retrieval contained the gold answer, answer CE cut instruction-form adoption from \cfStInAiR\% to \cfStInAiCE\%. In the \cfStOutAiPairsR{} and \cfStOutAiPairsCE{} pairs where it did not, adoption went from \cfStOutAiR\% to \cfStOutAiCE\% (difference \cfStOutAiD{} points, 95\% interval \cfStOutAiDCI). The two effects differ by \reStrataDiff{} points \reStrataDiffCI, an interval computed after the fact. With the gold in the retrieval, declarative adoption fell only from \cfStInAdR\% to \cfStInAdCE\%. This pattern fits how the answer targets were built. The CE target is an answer the retrieved evidence supports, so the loss rewards siding with the evidence against an instruction, and training never contained a plain claim. We did not test this explanation. Under the lenient F1 criterion, also declared in the protocol, all four contrasts point the same way and the gold-uptake difference shrinks to \cfFonePc{} points. A second pre-declared lenient criterion, an LLM judge of answer equivalence calibrated against labels written by another LLM rather than by people, gives the same four decisions; we report it only in the released results.

\subsection{Questions kept from the target keep their correct answer}
\label{sec:interference}
Answer CE did more than avoid the attacker's string. Had it only learned that, the attack would still turn correct answers into other wrong ones; $P_d$ tests this against the benign instruction (Table~\ref{tab:interference}). Under the override, the answer-CE agents kept \reCorrectOverrideCE\% of their clean-correct answers and the random-injection agents \reCorrectOverrideR\%. Under the pre-declared measure, the excess of other wrong answers fell by \cfPdAbs{} points \cfPdCI{} (seed-resampled \cfPdNested). Counted over all clean-correct pairs (secondary), the two configurations were similar (\reDoneDiff{} \reDoneDiffCI); the two counts differ because a hijacked answer cannot also be another wrong answer. How much non-target damage answer CE removes therefore depends on what the hijacked answers would otherwise have been, between none detectable and about 8 points.

\subsection{A multi-search 7B agent is barely moved by either form}
\label{sec:sevenb}
The released 7B agent was barely moved by either form (development evidence). It answered \sbCleanEM\% of the MuSiQue questions correctly with clean retrieval. A wrong answer stated as a fact moved \sbAd\% \sbAdCI{} of the \sbNcc{} questions it otherwise answered correctly. We had predicted at least 10\%, so the prediction was refuted. Phrased as an override instruction, the same wrong answer was adopted on \sbAi\% \sbAiCI, an instruction bonus of \sbBonus{} points \sbBonusCI, within the predicted 10 points. For our 3B agents the same bonus was \cfBonusR{} points under random injection and \cfBonusCE{} under answer CE. The nonsense target was adopted on \sbT\%. A correct answer inserted as an instruction was used on \sbU\% \sbUCI{} of the \sbNcw{} questions the agent otherwise answered wrongly, above the predicted 40\%, so the agent does not discount inserted text wholesale (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:sevenbpred}).

On the \scN{} questions of the search check, with the same corpus and retriever, the released single-search 3B ended with the nonsense target on \scThreeTarget\% of questions under the override attack, and the 7B on \scSevenTarget\% (exploratory; all questions, since the 3B answered only \scThreeCleanEM\% correctly). The two checkpoints differ in size and training as well as in how often they search, so this does not isolate the effect of searching again. The direction agrees with \citet{bi2026haegeo}, who find that more agentic search improves final resistance. The released 7B agent was rarely moved by either payload form, so a gate would have little to remove in this agent.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Answer supervision against retrieval injection teaches a single-search agent to resist instructions that push a wrong answer, halving adoption of a plausible one and nearly eliminating the attack's nonsense target, while it keeps following instructions that push the right answer at a cost of a few points. It barely changes how often the agent adopts the same wrong answer stated as a fact, so the defense leaves mostly open the channel that knowledge-poisoning attacks use. In a released multi-search 7B agent, studied as development evidence, neither form moved the agent much to begin with.

Attack success and clean accuracy show the gain against wrong instructions, but not the open declarative channel or the small cost to correct content. Two extra conditions show both, the attack's answer stated as a fact and the correct answer delivered as an instruction, and evaluations of injection defenses should report them.
